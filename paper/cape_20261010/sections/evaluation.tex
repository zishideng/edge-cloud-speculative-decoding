\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

We ask four questions. Is \cape{} faster than fixed windows and state-of-the-art pipelined systems when the network delay changes over time (Section~\ref{sec:main})? Does it match the best fixed window at every fixed delay and on every model pair, without being told either (Section~\ref{sec:fixed})? How does one weight trade speed for device energy (Section~\ref{sec:frontier})? And where do its gains come from (Section~\ref{sec:why})?

\subsection{Methodology}
\emph{Workloads.} Prompts are 50 GSM8K~\cite{gsm8k2021} grade-school math problems, 30 HumanEval~\cite{humaneval2021} Python function completions and the 30 first-turn questions of MT-Bench~\cite{mtbench2023}. Generation uses each model's chat template, greedy decoding and at most 512 output tokens.

\emph{Network conditions.} Every request runs under three conditions, on top of the real Wi-Fi path: a fixed added delay of 2\,ms (\emph{low}), a fixed added delay of 100\,ms (\emph{high}), and a \emph{dynamic} delay that switches between 2 and 100\,ms with exponentially distributed dwell times (mean 1.5\,s), as on a mobile link that moves between coverage conditions. The dynamic schedule is seeded per prompt, so every method faces exactly the same sequence of delays.

\emph{Protocol.} Each pair runs 110 prompts $\times$ 3 conditions $\times$ 10 methods, \E{x:requests} measured requests in total over the four pairs. Methods, prompts, seeds, conditions and analysis were frozen in a plan file before the first measured request, and methods are interleaved in a randomized, balanced order. Reference outputs and equal 16-token warm-ups are generated before timing starts. Throughput is the total number of output tokens divided by the total wall time, including terminal lookahead. Energy per token is the integrated board energy divided by the number of output tokens.

\emph{Methods.} All methods share the models, servers, token-ID transport and strict greedy verification; only the edge-side policy differs.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sync-4}: synchronous speculative decoding with four draft tokens per round and no lookahead.
\item \textbf{Fixed-$\gamma$}, $\gamma\in\{2,4,8\}$: $\gamma$ draft tokens per round with a complete-block lookahead of $h=\gamma+1$. \textbf{Best fixed} is the fastest of the three, chosen separately for every pair and fixed delay after the measurement, a hindsight oracle no deployed system can match.
\item \textbf{HF assisted}: assisted generation of Hugging Face Transformers~\cite{transformers2020} with its current defaults (synchronous, at most 20 draft tokens, adaptive confidence threshold).
\item \textbf{PipeSD}~\cite{pipesd2026}: continuous streamed drafting with confidence-triggered verification; its two thresholds are tuned for every pair and condition with the paper's budget of 16 trials.
\item \textbf{PicoSpec}~\cite{picospec2026}: optimistic drafting of the next window during verification with early interruption of background drafting on rejection, at the paper's default window of four tokens. Its fast pre-verification and probability compression require cloud changes or do not apply to greedy verification, and its truncation rule is not specified, so our implementation covers its edge-side pipeline. Under greedy drafting and verification, PicoSpec proposes and accepts exactly the tokens of Fixed-4 and differs from it only in timing, which makes it a clean measure of interruption.
\item \textbf{PEARL}~\cite{pearl2025}: parallel drafting and verification with pre- and post-verification; its draft length is calibrated for every pair and condition from a development run, as the paper prescribes.
\item \textbf{\cape{}} with $w=0$ (\capes{}, speed) and $w=0.5$ (\capee{}, energy). Both use the same settings for all pairs and conditions.
\end{itemize}
The baselines thus receive per-pair, per-condition calibration, and Best fixed receives hindsight; \cape{} receives neither.

\subsection{Time-varying delay: \cape{} is the fastest method on every pair}
\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:dynamic} reports the dynamic condition. \capes{} is faster than every other method on all four model pairs. It outruns the hindsight-optimal fixed window by \E{x:llama:dyn:S:abssp} on Llama, \E{x:qwen:dyn:S:abssp} on Qwen, \E{x:gemmatwo:dyn:S:abssp} on Gemma-2 and \E{x:gemmathree:dyn:S:abssp} on Gemma-3, and PEARL by \E{x:dyn:S:pearl:rng}. A fixed window, or a draft length calibrated for one delay, is right for only part of the request; \cape{} re-plans every round from the round trips it has just observed and follows the delay within a few rounds.

\cape{} is also frugal. On Llama, Qwen and Gemma-3, \capes{} is both faster and more energy-efficient than the best fixed window (\E{x:llama:dyn:S:abssv}, \E{x:qwen:dyn:S:abssv} and \E{x:gemmathree:dyn:S:abssv} lower J/token), and \capee{} lowers J/token by \E{x:dyn:E:sv:rng} at the speed of the best fixed window. Under time-varying delay, a \cape{} setting lies on the speed--energy frontier of every pair.

The pipelined systems show why per-condition calibration is not enough. PEARL and PipeSD are calibrated for the dynamic condition itself, yet a single draft length or threshold pair must serve both the 2\,ms and the 100\,ms phases of every request. Synchronous methods leave the device idle during every round trip: \capes{} is \E{x:sync:dyn:rng} faster than Sync-4 and \E{x:hf:dyn:rng} faster than HF assisted generation.

\subsection{Fixed delays: matching a hindsight oracle without knowing the delay}
\label{sec:fixed}
Table~\ref{tab:fixed} shows why no fixed window can be configured in advance: the best fixed window is Fixed-2, Fixed-4 or Fixed-8 depending on the pair and the delay. \capes{} needs no such choice. It beats the hindsight-optimal fixed window in \E{x:wins} of the 12 pair--delay settings, including all four pairs at 100\,ms (by \E{x:high:S:sp:rng}). At 100\,ms it is the fastest of all ten methods on Llama, Qwen and Gemma-3, and on Qwen it is also \E{x:qwen:high:S:abssv} more energy-efficient than the best fixed window.

At 2\,ms the round trip is short and every well-configured pipeline is close to its ceiling: \capes{} stays within a few percent of the best fixed window on every pair while the calibration-free alternatives Sync-4 and HF assisted generation lose \E{x:gemmatwo:low:Sync:abssp}--\E{x:gemmathree:low:HF:abssp}. Methods tuned specifically for this short round trip, such as PEARL with its calibrated draft length, are competitive here; their calibration does not carry over when the delay grows or varies (Table~\ref{tab:dynamic}). The heavy Gemma-2 draft (2B parameters) is the regime in which short windows are optimal at every delay; even there \capes{} beats the best fixed window at all three conditions.

\subsection{One weight selects the operating point}
\label{sec:frontier}
Table~\ref{tab:dynamic} also places every method in the speed--energy plane. Raising $w$ from 0 to 0.5 moves \cape{} along the frontier instead of off it: \capee{} gives up a little throughput relative to \capes{} and in return cuts J/token by \E{x:dyn:E:sv:rng} relative to the best fixed window, because the energy price shortens the lookahead whenever acceptance makes a hit unlikely. A deployment chooses its operating point with this single number and keeps it across model pairs and networks.

\subsection{Across the delay range}
\label{sec:sweep}
Fig.~\ref{fig:sweep} extends the two fixed delays to six, from 2 to 200\,ms, on the Gemma-3 pair. No fixed window stays best: Fixed-2 wins at 2\,ms, Fixed-4 at 10 and 25\,ms and Fixed-8 from 50\,ms on. \capes{} follows this envelope with one configuration. It is within \E{s:low:S:sub} of the best fixed window at 2\,ms, matches it at 10\,ms (\E{s:d10:S}) and is faster from 25\,ms on: by \E{s:d25:S} at 25\,ms, \E{s:d50:S} \E{s:d50:S:ci} at 50\,ms, \E{s:high:S:sub} at 100\,ms and \E{s:d200:S} at 200\,ms. Its largest gains fall exactly where the best window changes, between 50 and 100\,ms: there \cape{} combines short fresh windows after misses with long lookahead after hits, which no single window can express. Over the four added delays, \capes{} also uses \E{s:pooled:S:g4:sv} \E{s:pooled:S:g4:svci} less energy per token than Fixed-4 and \E{s:pooled:S:g8:sv} less than Fixed-8.

PEARL, whose draft length is recalibrated for every delay, is slower than \capes{} at all six delays; at 25\,ms its calibrated length is too short and it falls \E{s:d25:pearl} behind the best fixed window. The lookahead cap of $\Gamma+1=9$ tokens is ample: at 200\,ms, where PEARL drafts 12 tokens per block, \capes{} is still the fastest method.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates the components of \capes{} on the Gemma-3 pair, and each has a distinct job. \emph{Adaptive lookahead delivers the speed.} Keeping the fresh window at four tokens and letting only the lookahead follow the round trip already raises throughput by \E{a:fresh4:sp} \E{a:fresh4:spci} over Fixed-4, and by \E{a:fresh4:high:sp} at 100\,ms, where a complete-block lookahead leaves most of the round trip unused. \emph{The adaptive fresh window delivers the energy.} Choosing the fresh window as well keeps the speed (a further \E{a:fresh:sp}, within noise) and cuts J/token by another \E{a:fresh:sv} \E{a:fresh:svci}, and by \E{a:fresh:low:sv} at 2\,ms, where \cape{} drafts short blocks instead of speculating four tokens at a time. \emph{The distributional round-trip estimate prices jitter.} Compared with a median-only estimate, it runs at the same speed (within \E{a:dist:sp}) and lowers J/token by \E{a:dist:sv} \E{a:dist:svci}, and by \E{a:dist:high:sv} at 100\,ms: on a jittery path it stops buying lookahead that the shorter round trips cannot hide. The full controller is \E{a:full:sp} \E{a:full:spci} faster than Fixed-4 and \E{a:full:sv} \E{a:full:svci} more energy-efficient.

The text matters as much as the network: on Gemma-3, per-token acceptance ranges from \E{acc:gemmathree:mtbench} on MT-Bench to \E{acc:gemmathree:humaneval} on HumanEval, so no single window suits every workload, and \cape{} estimates acceptance online in each state. Short lookahead also limits waiting for background drafts that a rejection has made useless: at 2\,ms such waiting takes \E{wait:capes:low} of the wall time of \capes{}, against \E{wait:pico:low} for PicoSpec, which interrupts these drafts, and \E{wait:fixed4:low} for Fixed-4. Interruption is orthogonal to the choice of windows and composes with \cape{}.
