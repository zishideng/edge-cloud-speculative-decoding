\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) increasingly run close to their users, on phones, robots and embedded boards, for responsiveness, privacy and cost. Such devices can host only small models, while the quality users expect comes from models with billions of parameters running in the cloud. Speculative decoding~\cite{leviathan2023,chen2023} offers a natural split between the two. A small draft model proposes several tokens, and the large target model checks all of them in one forward pass. With greedy verification every emitted token is the target model's own greedy choice, yet each target pass can emit several tokens. Placing the draft model on the device and the target model in the cloud combines local responsiveness with cloud-level quality, and several recent systems explore this split~\cite{sled2025,specedge2025,dsd2025,picospec2026,pipesd2026,wisp2026}.

The split exposes a cost that single-machine speculative decoding does not have: every verification is a network round trip, and in synchronous speculative decoding the device sits idle through each of them. \emph{Lookahead} turns this idle time into progress: while the cloud verifies the current block, the device already drafts the next one, assuming that the current block will be accepted~\cite{pearl2025,picospec2026,pipesd2026}. How much to draft, and when, is the crux. Tokens drafted before a request is sent delay it; tokens drafted during verification are free in time but are discarded whenever the verifier rejects the speculated prefix, and on a battery-powered device every discarded token is wasted energy. The best balance moves with the network delay, the draft speed and how predictable the text is. In our measurements, the fastest fixed window is two, four or eight tokens depending on the model pair and the delay, and each of these windows is no faster than synchronous decoding in at least one setting. Real access networks change their delay from second to second, so any setting tuned offline for one condition is mistuned for the next.

We study this problem on a real deployment: an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano drafts over campus Wi-Fi for a cloud verifier served by vLLM~\cite{vllm2023}, with the board's input power measured for every request. The loop has a simple asymmetry (Section~\ref{sec:measure}):
\emph{Tokens drafted after a verification returns delay the next request, whereas tokens drafted during verification are nearly free in time but not in energy.} A good controller should therefore draft as little as possible on the critical path, and draft ahead only as much as is likely to be reused within the round trip the network actually delivers.

We present \cape{} (Critical-path-Aware PrEfetching), an edge-only controller built on this principle. \cape{} models the decoding pipeline as a two-state Markov chain whose states record whether the current round can reuse a prefetched block (a hit) or must draft afresh (a miss). For every pair of fresh draft window $\gamma_f$ and lookahead length $h$, the model predicts the long-run time, draft-busy time and number of emitted tokens per round. It prices the overlap of drafting and verification with the recent distribution of round trips rather than a single mean, and it prices the interruption of lookahead that a rejection has made useless. \cape{} picks the pair that minimizes an energy-weighted cost per token, where a single weight $w$ trades speed against energy. All inputs are measured online, so \cape{} needs no offline calibration for a new model pair, device or network. It keeps strict greedy verification and requires no change to the cloud server.

We evaluate \cape{} on four draft/target pairs from three model families (Llama-3, Qwen2.5, Gemma-2 and Gemma-3), on 110 prompts from GSM8K, HumanEval and MT-Bench, under a 2\,ms delay, a 100\,ms delay and a delay that switches between the two over time: \E{n:main} paired requests against seven baselines, including synchronous speculative decoding and the pipelined systems PEARL~\cite{pearl2025}, PicoSpec~\cite{picospec2026} and PipeSD~\cite{pipesd2026}, which are calibrated offline for every pair and delay. \capes{} is \E{x:S:sync:rng} faster than synchronous speculative decoding in all twelve pair--delay settings, with every 95\% interval above zero. Under time-varying delay it is \E{x:S:sync:dyn:rng} faster, and \cape{} is the fastest of all nine methods on every pair. One configuration serves all twelve settings: \capes{} beats the fixed window chosen in hindsight for each setting in \E{x:S:best:wins} of them and is never more than \E{x:S:regret} behind the fastest method of any setting, whereas every baseline falls at least \E{x:base:regret} behind in some setting. Across six delays from 2 to 200\,ms, \capes{} follows the upper envelope of all fixed windows and is faster than PEARL at every delay. The energy setting \capee{} matches the speed of the hindsight-best fixed window under time-varying delay and uses up to \E{gemma3:dyn:E:best:svm} less device energy per token; on Gemma-3 it is both faster and more energy-efficient than synchronous decoding at every delay.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cost asymmetry.} We identify that speculative work in edge--cloud decoding is asymmetric: fresh drafting occupies the critical path, while drafting during verification is nearly free in time but not in energy, and we price both with a board energy model of wall time and draft-busy time (Section~\ref{sec:measure}).
\item \textbf{Controller.} \cape{} is the first controller that jointly sizes an on-path fresh window and an off-path lookahead every round. Its two-state Markov model prices the round-trip distribution, the interruption of invalid lookahead and the device energy of every speculative token. It needs no training, no calibration, no draft probabilities and no cloud changes, and it decides in \E{ctl:decide} on the device CPU (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item \textbf{Cross-family evaluation on real hardware.} On a Jetson Orin Nano and a real Wi-Fi path, we report throughput and device energy per token for nine methods, four model pairs and three network conditions. \cape{} is faster than synchronous speculative decoding in every setting, the fastest method under time-varying delay on every pair, and the fast end of the speed--energy frontier there (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}
